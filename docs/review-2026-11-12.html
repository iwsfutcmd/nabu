Your questions

Bug · planner

Why later lessons keep asking “Thank you”

It’s a bug in how a lesson pulls in older material. When the planner builds a lesson, it adds up to six earlier sentences that have a word due for review. It takes them in course order and keeps the first six (packages/core/src/learner/planner.ts:207-208). Unit 1’s sentences are full of the most common words, so one of their words is nearly always due, and unit 1 wins every time.

In a simulated apc learner, lessons from unit 3 onward spent 42% of their questions on earlier-unit sentences (239 of 572 first tries). The most repeated were all intro greetings:

Sentence (intro unit)Times asked in unit 3+ lessons
mni7, el7amdella. (fine, thank God)21
ana mni7. (I’m fine)17
enta mni7? (are you fine?)15
ana Sami. (I’m Sami)14

Every course behaves this way. In es, every review item inside a lesson came from units 1 and 2. Material from unit 3 onward is never reviewed inside a lesson, so it fades unless you open Review yourself.

The fix is small: pick review sentences by need (the most forgotten words first, spread across units, not the same sentence lesson after lesson) and cap review at a share of the lesson. Whether lessons should contain review at all is decision D1. See finding L1.

Today’s design

Spaced repetition in Nabu today

There is one spaced-repetition algorithm, plus two simpler ways missed items come back.

  • FSRS-6, through the ts-fsrs library (5.4.2), in packages/core/src/learner/state.ts. Every word or concept has a separate memory card per skill: recognising, producing, listening, speaking, and reading and writing the script (script cards are kept per writing system). Ratings come from how you answered: wrong or revealed is Again; a typo, a missing accent or a slow answer is Hard; otherwise Good. Easy is never given, and you only rate yourself on unscored speaking cards. A card is rated at most once per session. It aims for 90% recall with the library’s default settings and no random jitter. Nothing tunes those settings per learner yet (per-learner tuning is Phase 14 in docs/next-steps.md).
  • The mistakes list: your last 50 missed exercises, practised from the course page. It has no timing, so it isn’t spaced repetition.
  • Retry within a lesson: a missed exercise comes back at the end of the same lesson.

A separate performance model (performance.ts, Elo-style: your ability against each item’s difficulty) predicts your chance of getting each exercise right. It doesn’t schedule anything; the planner uses it to aim each session at about 85% success.

Your idea · adaptive pacing

Should fast learners move faster and strugglers get more repetition?

Today nothing adapts the pace. A lesson opens as soon as the one before it is finished, however it went. A lesson is 10 to 14 exercises for everyone. Review sessions only happen if you open them. What does adapt is which exercise types a lesson picks (easier ones for weaker learners) and each card’s FSRS interval.

Thirty simulated days of an es learner who always takes the next lesson, by hidden ability (θ):

Ability θFirst-try successLessons doneWords metRecognised and producedCards dueLesson time spent on review
0.5 (weak)82.2%60251826950%
1.086.2%6025111128738%
2.086.0%6025111738038%
3.0 (strong)89.2%6025122347922%

“Cards due” counts word-skill cards whose recall has dropped below 90%. It is the number the course page shows next to Review. “Lesson time spent on review” is the share of a lesson’s first tries on earlier material, almost all of it from unit 1 (finding L1).

So every learner meets new material at the same speed. The weak learner falls further behind every day without being slowed down, and the strong learner is held to the same pace while their review pile grows anyway.

Proposal

  1. A pace signal, computed from the event log like everything else (a pure function, no stored state). It has three inputs: the backlog of words met but not yet known (which needs the “known” rule below, so the two ideas go together), the overdue review load, and how your recent first tries compare with what the model predicted. It sorts you into three bands, consolidate, steady and accelerate, with some hysteresis so you don’t flip between them every session.
  2. What each band changes:
    • The main Continue action. When the backlog is high, it opens a practice session before the next new lesson; “New lesson anyway” always stays available (D6).
    • Lesson make-up: the share of review (for example about 10%, 20% or 35%) and how many exercises each new word gets.
    • The retention target, which plan() already accepts but the app never sets (for example 0.87, 0.90 or 0.93): less review for fast learners, more for those who struggle.
    • Ratings: stop calling half of all correct answers Hard (L7), and allow Easy for fast, sure production (L8).
    • Skipping ahead: when accelerating, offer “Test out of the rest of this unit”, reusing the adaptive placement test (ADR 0077) for one unit, and seed the skipped words (L9).
  3. A guard: extend the simulator with learners who differ in learning speed as well as ability, with varied answer times, following Continue. Check that success stays at 85% ± 5 for everyone, the backlog stays bounded, strong learners reach “known” sooner and weak learners know more words by day 30 (L12).
  4. An ADR for the pacing rules and the “known” definition, since both change the planner (§9.4).

Your idea · when a word is known

A word counts as known only once it has been recognised and produced (and heard, where the voice is good)

Today there is no such rule. The word list counts a word as learned once any of its cards has a review, and FSRS counts a wrong answer as a review (words.ts:46-55). Elsewhere, the planner treats a word as fine to use in other sentences once it has been seen at all.

The real app differs from the simulation (see How this was checked), but the gap is structural: the planner has no goal of getting each word produced or heard.

Proposal

  • Definition. A word or concept (word, verb form, affix or construction) is known once you’ve got each required skill right on a first try:
    • recognise: any reading-to-meaning card, stories included;
    • produce: you supply the form yourself, typed, or spoken from the meaning once scored speech exists. That’s the modality-agnostic part.
    • hear: only where the course’s voice is rated good and that word has a clip that ships (es, fa, uz, apc and yue today; not hbo or tok, which are iffy, not grc, which is bad, and not lzh, which is never heard). A word whose clip is held back for review can’t be required to be heard.
  • Where it lives. In the fold (state.ts), the first successful demonstration is recorded per card, so it stays a projection of the log. Words move from new to learning to known, and their recall strength is still shown separately, as it is now.
  • What uses it. The word list (known and learning, with counts), mastery per unit on the path, the planner’s targets (each learning word’s missing skills, L5) and the pace signal above.
  • Open questions: D2 (does a success in the same session you met the word count?), D3 (does forgetting it later demote it?) and D4 (which exercise types count as production?).

Your concern · code review

Code that only works for one course

The reviewer grepped every TypeScript and Python source file for course ids, language tags and reading-scheme names. Most language knowledge already sits behind data: orthography profiles, named normalization steps, the per-script punctuation table, the analyzer registry and per-voice config. The refactors that remove most of this, biggest payoff first:

  1. Reading schemes as data with one plug-in interface (C3). This serves Mandarin, Japanese furigana, Vietnamese, Thai and Korean.
  2. Speech and audio-check settings in the profile and course.yaml, shipped in the manifest (C6). This unblocks es speech scoring now.
  3. Script facts from Unicode and CLDR rather than lists in code, with profile exemplars for private-use scripts (C7, C2).
  4. Script tracks driven by the profile (C8), opening alphabet tracks for hbo, fa, apc, uz Cyrillic and Devanagari.
  5. Course and orthography names in course.yaml (C19).

Performance checks · measured

Fast for new learners, slow for long-time ones

For a new learner the app is quick: lessons open in about 100 ms and grading passes its 5 ms budget. With a year of simulated daily es practice (29,674 events, 2,821 cards) it slows badly, because several paths re-read and re-process the whole history on every answer. What to keep

The reviews also recorded what works well. Keep these as they are while fixing the rest.

  • Lessons: the pure player state machine and its IME handling (with a real-composition e2e test); verdicts that tell typos, missing marks, variants and wrong words apart, with notes on endings that carry meaning; the persons tags; the word bank’s fixed slots, 44px targets, tap, drag, keys and Backspace; preloaded clips, 0.75× slow audio with the pitch kept, clips that wait for the feedback tone; Check and Continue kept in reach on phones; option numbers in Persian and Arabic digits.
  • Around the lessons: Start over as a typed confirmation and a progress_reset event; password-confirmed export and deletion; offline downloads per unit with sizes and checksums; a mistakes count of only what can be practised now; one-tap undo of a listening break; word-list search that ignores vowel marks and waits for IME composition; story tap-for-gloss and translation toggles; honest draft notices; the theme applied before first paint; text size that scales only the language being learned.
  • Motivation and AI: the gate off by default and never interrupting a lesson; streaks that handle late syncs, the date line and daylight saving, with automatic freezes and no guilt copy; reminder caps (one a day, none after practice, backing off when ignored); AI that degrades cleanly with no model, labels its explanations and treats learner text as data; reports that send no ids; leagues opt-in per instance, without bots; recordings never stored.
  • In use: lessons open in about 100 ms, and a throttled cold load reaches Home in 3.1 s; no horizontal overflow at 390px in any of the nine courses; Persian right to left renders correctly; Jyutping ruby on by default and lzh’s reading picker; placement is short (12 questions) and placed sensibly; sign-up has clear errors and a confusable-username guard.
  • Code patterns to copy: language needs as named normalization steps in the profile; punctuation by ISO 15924 script code with language overrides; font stacks from profile, then script, then UI default; the analyzer registry keyed by the profile; per-voice behaviour as config; one TypeScript grader that Python calls, with conformance vectors for deliberate twins; pure core logic with injected clock and RNG.

How this was checked

  • Learning model and pacing (Claude): read packages/core/src/learner and lesson, then ran the repo’s own 30-day learner simulator (packages/core/test/simulator.ts) on the committed bundles, and ts-fsrs directly for the rating comparison. Scripts are in the job folder and aren’t part of the repo.
  • Simulator caveats: the simulated learner always takes the next lesson and never opens Review, answers in a constant 4 seconds (so it never triggers the “slow” rule), and its abilities are relative to the simulator’s own item difficulties. Treat the numbers as the shape of the problem, not exact predictions of real learners.
  • Other reviews: six reviewers ran in parallel, each limited to one area: the lesson player, the app shell and onboarding, motivation and AI features, a hands-on walkthrough in a real browser, code quality (with a focus on course-specific code) and performance (measured).
